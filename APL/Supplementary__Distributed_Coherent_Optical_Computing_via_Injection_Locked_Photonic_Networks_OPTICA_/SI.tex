\begin{abstract}
This supplementary material provides additional methodological details and supporting simulation results for the primary manuscript. We describe the empirical criteria used to define the locking-region boundary, the Fourier-domain extraction procedure used to calculate modulation responses, and additional simulations showing how the locking maps depend on linewidth enhancement factor and injected-laser operating threshold. We also include time-domain AM and PM modulation traces to illustrate the extraction of AM$\rightarrow$AM, AM$\rightarrow$PM, PM$\rightarrow$AM, and PM$\rightarrow$PM response channels and to visualize nonlinear behavior at large phase-modulation depth.
\end{abstract}

\section{Supplementary Methods}

\subsection{Defining the Locking Range}

After plotting the parameter maps of $\Delta I/\bar{I}$ and $\Delta\phi$, a fairly clear transition can be observed in both cases: one side corresponds to a low-value region with comparatively steady behavior, whereas the other corresponds to a region with increased residual oscillation or phase drift. To better identify the approximate range of this transition, we tested a series of threshold values and found that the boundary in the present simulations lies roughly near 0.1 for the intensity metric and 0.1~rad for the phase metric. Accordingly, we use $\Delta I/\bar{I}\le 0.10$ and $\Delta\phi\le 0.10~\mathrm{rad}$ as empirical operational thresholds to help delineate the boundary of the locking region. For the intensity metric, $\Delta I/\bar{I}\le 0.10$ indicates residual variation below approximately 10\% of the mean intensity, which in this work generally separates approximately steady trajectories from states with more pronounced oscillation. For the phase metric, $\Delta\phi\le 0.10~\mathrm{rad}$ similarly corresponds to a small residual phase excursion over the analysis window, indicating that the field phase remains approximately stationary. These numerical values are therefore not intended as strict theoretical criteria for injection locking, but rather as empirical thresholds used to more clearly identify and outline the locking-region boundary in the present simulations. This distinction is relevant because prior studies have shown that optically injected lasers can exhibit oscillatory states with bounded phase and average-frequency entrainment near Hopf-mediated locking transitions, even when the intensity is no longer strictly steady-state~\cite{Kelleher2012PRE_BoundedPhase,Pausch2012NJP_QDInjectionDynamics}. In that sense, the present thresholds are used to define a conservative practical operating window emphasizing jointly stable amplitude and phase behavior, rather than the broadest possible notion of frequency locking.
\textcolor{red}{The cutoff values apply to the intensity and phase metrics themselves, rather than fractions of the plotted color scale. Their effect on the joint-region boundary is evaluated in Sec.~IV.A.}
\textcolor{red}{For the unmodulated maps, we sweep injection ratio from $-60$ to $0$~dB in 1~dB steps and detuning from $-30$ to $30$~GHz in 100~MHz steps. Each trajectory starts from $E(0)=0$ and $N(0)=N_{\mathrm{th}}=N_0+1/(g_0\tau_s)$, with $R_p=1.15N_{\mathrm{th}}/\tau_n$ and the coupling factor specified in the main text. We integrate the rate equations using fourth-order Runge--Kutta with a 0.2~ps step for 90,000 steps (18~ns), and evaluate the intensity and unwrapped-phase metrics over the final 3,000 samples (0.6~ns). The constant injection field is $E_{\mathrm{inj}}=A_{\mathrm{fr}}10^{R_{\mathrm{inj,dB}}/20}$, where $A_{\mathrm{fr}}$ is the free-running field amplitude from the same model.}

\subsection{Fourier-Domain Extraction of Modulation Response}


For each operating point $(\Delta\nu, R_{\mathrm{inj}}, f_{\mathrm{mod}})$, the rate equations are first integrated through a transient interval, and the modulation response is extracted from the steady-state measurement window. The simulated injected-laser field is written as
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{red}{E_{\mathrm{injected}}}(t)=\mathrm{Re}\{\textcolor{red}{E_{\mathrm{injected}}}(t)\}+i\mathrm{Im}\{\textcolor{red}{E_{\mathrm{injected}}}(t)\}.
\end{equation}

The amplitude response channel is defined as the normalized field-amplitude fluctuation,
\begin{equation}
a_s(t)=\frac{|\textcolor{red}{E_{\mathrm{injected}}}(t)|}{\langle |\textcolor{red}{E_{\mathrm{injected}}}(t)| \rangle}-1,
\end{equation}
where $\langle \cdot \rangle$ denotes the time average over the measurement window. The phase response channel is obtained from the optical phase,
\begin{equation}
\phi_s(t)=\mathrm{unwrap}\left[\arg(\textcolor{red}{E_{\mathrm{injected}}}(t))\right].
\end{equation}
For the modulation-response extraction, the unwrapped phase traces are linearly detrended over the measurement window to remove any residual average phase drift before extracting the modulation component.

The fundamental response amplitude at the imposed modulation frequency is extracted by projecting each input and output waveform onto the sine and cosine components at $f_{\mathrm{mod}}$. Specifically, for a waveform $y(t)$ sampled over the measurement window, the amplitude at $f_{\mathrm{mod}}$ is obtained from the quadrature components at angular frequency $2\pi f_{\mathrm{mod}}$. This procedure is equivalent to extracting the Fourier component at the imposed modulation frequency and avoids using broadband spectral features unrelated to the driven response. The resulting input and output amplitudes are denoted as $A_{\mathrm{in}}(f_{\mathrm{mod}})$ and $A_{\mathrm{out}}(f_{\mathrm{mod}})$, respectively.

The response is reported in decibels as
\begin{equation}
H_{\mathrm{dB}}(f_{\mathrm{mod}})
=
20\log_{10}
\left[
\frac{
A_{\mathrm{out}}(f_{\mathrm{mod}})
}{
A_{\mathrm{in}}(f_{\mathrm{mod}})
}
\right].
\end{equation}

For AM-driven injection, $A_{\mathrm{in}}$ is extracted from the normalized remote amplitude modulation. For PM-driven injection, $A_{\mathrm{in}}$ is extracted from the unwrapped and linearly detrended remote phase modulation. The same procedure is applied to the corresponding AM and PM output channels to obtain the AM$\rightarrow$AM, AM$\rightarrow$PM, PM$\rightarrow$AM, and PM$\rightarrow$PM responses.

\section{Time-Domain Illustration of Modulation Channels}

Figure~\ref{fig:supp-am-pm-time} illustrates the time-domain input and output channels used for extracting the modulation response. The traces are calculated at a representative operating point with $\alpha=5$, $R_{\mathrm{inj}}=-50~\mathrm{dB}$, zero detuning, and $f_{\mathrm{mod}}=2~\mathrm{GHz}$. After a transient interval, the last eight modulation cycles are plotted for modulation depths from 0.60 to 1.00. For AM-driven injection, the input channel is the normalized source-field amplitude modulation, and the output channels are the normalized injected-laser field amplitude and the injected-laser field phase. These channels correspond to the AM$\rightarrow$AM and AM$\rightarrow$PM responses. For PM-driven injection, the input channel is the source-field phase modulation with depth $m_\phi=0.6\pi$ to $1.0\pi$, and the output channels are again the injected-laser field amplitude and phase, corresponding to the PM$\rightarrow$AM and PM$\rightarrow$PM responses. Thus, the figure provides a direct time-domain visualization of the channels used in the Fourier-domain extraction procedure described above.

This figure also provides additional detail for the nonlinear phase-modulation behavior discussed in the main text. In the small-signal regime, increasing modulation depth would primarily scale the response amplitude. However, for PM modulation depths of approximately $0.6\pi$ and above, the PM$\rightarrow$AM and PM$\rightarrow$PM traces show changes in waveform shape and relative amplitude that are not simple proportional rescalings. These time-domain traces therefore help visualize the onset of nonlinear response at large PM depth.
\textcolor{red}{For these traces, the dimensionless PM depth $m_\phi=\Delta\phi/\pi$ ranges from 0.6 to 1.0, corresponding to peak phase excursions of $0.6\pi$--$1.0\pi$.}

\subsection{\textcolor{red}{Complex-Field Readout and Facet Reflection}}
\textcolor{red}{For Fig.~\ref{fig:review-reflection}, we retain the modulation sequence and timing of main-text Fig.~6. Independent random generators with seeds 4 and 8 produce the remote and local inputs, respectively. The zero-order-held waveforms pass through a 20~GHz Gaussian FIR filter and are sampled at 64 points per encoded symbol. The active sequence contains 200 encoded symbols, of which the first 100 are displayed.}

\textcolor{red}{We represent the field incident on the local remodulator as the sum of the rate-equation output and a coherent reflected component:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
E_{\mathrm{out}}(t)&=E_{\mathrm{injected}}(t)+\sqrt{R_f}E_{\mathrm{inj}}(t),\\
E_{\mathrm{injected}}^{(\mathrm{modulated})}(t)
&=\frac{E_{\mathrm{out}}(t)}{\langle E_{\mathrm{injected}}\rangle_{\mathrm{CW}}}\,y(t).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The power reflectivity is $R_f=0$ or 0.30, giving a reflected-field coefficient $\sqrt{R_f}$. The injected field is $E_{\mathrm{inj}}=A_{\mathrm{fr}}10^{R_{\mathrm{inj,dB}}/20}[1+x(t)]$, and the signed computational data field is $E_{\mathrm{remote}}^{(\mathrm{modulated})}=x(t)$. We normalize both reflection cases by the complex mean of the no-reflection field over 10--19~ns, before data modulation. This fixed calibration is independent of the input data. Both fields are expressed in effective output-plane units with unit intracavity-to-output conversion. The laser parameters are held fixed in this comparison; quantitative predictions for a particular device require calibration of facet coupling and output conversion.}

\textcolor{red}{The balanced-detector output and its accumulation are evaluated at every time sample:
\begin{equation}
D_j=2\operatorname{Re}\!\left\{x_j\left[E_{\mathrm{injected},j}^{(\mathrm{modulated})}\right]^*\right\},
\qquad A_n=\frac{1}{64}\sum_{j=1}^{n}D_j,
\end{equation}
where sampling begins at the onset of data modulation at 20~ns. At symbol boundaries, $A_n$ equals the sum of the preceding symbol-averaged products. The ideal reference is $D_j^{\mathrm{ideal}}=2x_jy_j$, evaluated using the same filtered inputs. The display transformation $(1+\operatorname{Re}E)/2$ in main-text Fig.~6b is not used in the readout calculation. A reflected contribution can reinforce or partly cancel the existing error for a given input; the statistical comparison below assesses its effect across multiple inputs.}

\subsection{\textcolor{red}{Accumulated-Error Statistics over 1000 Inputs}}
\textcolor{red}{We evaluate $Q=1000$ independent remote/local input pairs with 1024 active encoded symbols per vector. The same pairs are used for all combinations of $\alpha=0,2,5$, injection ratios of $-50$, $-40$, and $-30$~dB, and $R_f=0$ or 0.30, at zero detuning. Remote and local vectors are generated independently. The inputs retain the 100\% field-modulation depth, eight-level magnitudes, antipodal encoding, filtering, sampling, and 20~ns CW settling interval used above. Results at length $L$ are evaluated from the first $L$ symbols of each trajectory.}

\textcolor{red}{For input pair $q$, we define the accumulated error and ensemble root-mean-square error as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\epsilon_q(L)&=A_{\mathrm{OIL},q}(L)-A_{\mathrm{ideal},q}(L),\\
A_{\mathrm{OIL},q}(L)&=\frac{1}{64}\sum_{j=1}^{64L}D_{q,j},\qquad
\mathrm{RMSE}(L)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{Q}\sum_q\epsilon_q(L)^2}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Relative to the ideal readout defined in Sec.~IV.D, these errors isolate the contributions of OIL and reflection. They exclude the difference between filtered-waveform products and the original digital dot product.}

\textcolor{red}{The final errors in Fig.~\ref{fig:review-statistics} are expressed as percentages of a common ideal-output RMS:
\begin{equation}
S=\sqrt{\frac{1}{Q}\sum_q A_{\mathrm{ideal},q}(1024)^2},
\qquad p_q=100\frac{\epsilon_q(1024)}{S}.
\label{eq:final-error-normalization}
\end{equation}
Here $S$ is the RMS of the 1000 ideal accumulated outputs. Using this common denominator for every parameter combination preserves the shape of each error distribution and avoids division by individual ideal outputs near zero. The percentage is therefore relative to the ensemble RMS, rather than each input's ideal result. Gaussian fits characterize the observed distributions without assuming Gaussian statistics.}

\textcolor{red}{To compare accuracy across accumulation lengths, Fig.~\ref{fig:review-statistics-metrics} reports the normalized RMSE and Pearson correlation:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{NRMSE}(L)&=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_q\epsilon_q(L)^2}{\sum_q A_{\mathrm{ideal},q}(L)^2}},\\
r_{\mathrm P}(L)&=\operatorname{corr}_q[A_{\mathrm{OIL},q}(L),A_{\mathrm{ideal},q}(L)].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
NRMSE is plotted as a percentage of the ideal-output RMS at each $L$. Correlation measures linear association across input pairs and is insensitive to an additive offset or a positive overall gain; it therefore complements, but does not replace, the error metric.}

\textcolor{red}{The dependence on $\alpha$ follows from amplitude--phase coupling and the phase sensitivity of coherent detection. Writing the calibrated field without reflection as $E_{\mathrm{injected}}/\langle E_{\mathrm{injected}}\rangle_{\mathrm{CW}}=(1+a_\alpha)e^{i\phi_\alpha}$, we obtain
\begin{equation}
\delta D_\alpha=2xy[(1+a_\alpha)\cos\phi_\alpha-1]
\simeq 2xy\left(a_\alpha-\frac{\phi_\alpha^2}{2}\right).
\end{equation}
The residual amplitude and phase fluctuations are denoted by $a_\alpha$ and $\phi_\alpha$, respectively. Near the calibrated operating point, amplitude errors enter at first order, while phase errors enter at second order. Amplitude--phase coupling~\cite{Lau2009InjectionTutorial} can therefore change the relative contributions of these terms to the detected error. In the simulated cases, nonzero $\alpha$ reduces RMSE at $-50$ and $-40$~dB, whereas larger errors occur at $-30$~dB, particularly for $\alpha=5$. This expansion describes the small-residual sensitivity; the reported results use the full nonlinear rate equations and exact readout.}

\section{\textcolor{red}{Short-Reach Implementation and Stabilization}}
\label{sec:short-reach}
\textcolor{red}{For the short-reach links considered here, practical operation requires control of both the laser operating point and the relative phase and polarization at the detector. The following measures outline a possible implementation.}

\textcolor{red}{The allowable frequency drift depends on the operating detuning and the range over which locking and acceptable modulation suppression coexist. It cannot be inferred from telecom-grade laser specifications alone. The static maps identify candidate operating regions but do not determine a quantitative drift tolerance.}

\textcolor{red}{Combined OIL--OPLL systems use optical injection to suppress rapid relative-phase fluctuations and electronic feedback to compensate slower drift, extending the system hold-in range~\cite{Bordonalli1999OIPLL}. In the proposed implementation, a filtered carrier-selective monitor or dedicated pilot would provide an error signal for PID control of the injected-laser current or temperature. This avoids relying on the data-bearing sidebands as a locking reference. A separate phase servo before interference would compensate differential optical-path fluctuations; it would not replace control of the laser operating point. Loop bandwidths and residual errors under data modulation require experimental validation.}

\textcolor{red}{Polarization-maintaining fiber with aligned launch axes, or active polarization tracking, would preserve injection coupling and polarization overlap at the detector. Acoustic and thermal path-length fluctuations would be addressed through compact routing, environmental isolation, and phase feedback within the control bandwidth. Dispersion can be reduced by shortening the link or using compatible 1310~nm devices near the zero-dispersion wavelength of conventional single-mode fiber~\cite{CorningSMF28Ultra}, with the laser and link parameters recalibrated for that wavelength. Where the power budget permits, omitting in-line amplification also avoids amplifier-added noise. Residual laser noise, loss, and fluctuations beyond the control bandwidth must be characterized for the chosen link; the present calculations do not establish a maximum transmission distance or a complete network-noise budget.}
