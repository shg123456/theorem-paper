\begin{abstract}
This supplementary material provides additional methodological details and supporting simulation results for the primary manuscript. We describe the empirical criteria used to define the locking-region boundary, the Fourier-domain extraction procedure used to calculate modulation responses, and additional simulations showing how the locking maps depend on linewidth enhancement factor and injected-laser operating threshold. We also include time-domain AM and PM modulation traces to illustrate the extraction of AM$\rightarrow$AM, AM$\rightarrow$PM, PM$\rightarrow$AM, and PM$\rightarrow$PM response channels and to visualize nonlinear behavior at large phase-modulation depth.
\end{abstract}

\section{Supplementary Methods}

\subsection{Defining the Locking Range}

After plotting the parameter maps of $\Delta I/\bar{I}$ and $\Delta\phi$, a fairly clear transition can be observed in both cases: one side corresponds to a low-value region with comparatively steady behavior, whereas the other corresponds to a region with increased residual oscillation or phase drift. To better identify the approximate range of this transition, we tested a series of threshold values and found that the boundary in the present simulations lies roughly near 0.1 for the intensity metric and 0.1~rad for the phase metric. Accordingly, we use $\Delta I/\bar{I}\le 0.10$ and $\Delta\phi\le 0.10~\mathrm{rad}$ as empirical operational thresholds to help delineate the boundary of the locking region. For the intensity metric, $\Delta I/\bar{I}\le 0.10$ indicates residual variation below approximately 10\% of the mean intensity, which in this work generally separates approximately steady trajectories from states with more pronounced oscillation. For the phase metric, $\Delta\phi\le 0.10~\mathrm{rad}$ similarly corresponds to a small residual phase excursion over the analysis window, indicating that the field phase remains approximately stationary. These numerical values are therefore not intended as strict theoretical criteria for injection locking, but rather as empirical thresholds used to more clearly identify and outline the locking-region boundary in the present simulations. This distinction is relevant because prior studies have shown that optically injected lasers can exhibit oscillatory states with bounded phase and average-frequency entrainment near Hopf-mediated locking transitions, even when the intensity is no longer strictly steady-state~\cite{Kelleher2012PRE_BoundedPhase,Pausch2012NJP_QDInjectionDynamics}. In that sense, the present thresholds are used to define a conservative practical operating window emphasizing jointly stable amplitude and phase behavior, rather than the broadest possible notion of frequency locking.
\textcolor{red}{The cutoffs describe the visible dark--bright transition, not a rigorous stability boundary or a fraction of the color-bar range. Reducing both metric cutoffs from 0.10 to 0.01 changes the joint-region area by only 0.23\% for $\alpha=0$ and 0.96\% for $\alpha=5$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:review-threshold}). The same trajectories and finite observation window are used throughout.}
\textcolor{red}{The archived unmodulated maps used for this cutoff comparison span $-60$ to $0$~dB in 1~dB steps and $-30$ to $30$~GHz in 100~MHz steps. The corresponding rate-equation implementation initializes each point with $E(0)=0$ and $N(0)=N_{\mathrm{th}}=N_0+1/(g_0\tau_s)$, with $R_p=1.15N_{\mathrm{th}}/\tau_n$ and $\kappa_{\mathrm{scale}}=1$. Fourth-order Runge--Kutta integration uses a 0.2~ps step for 90,000 steps (18~ns); the final 3,000 samples (0.6~ns) provide the intensity and unwrapped-phase metrics. The constant injected field is $E_{\mathrm{inj}}=A_{\mathrm{fr}}10^{R_{\mathrm{inj,dB}}/20}$, where $A_{\mathrm{fr}}$ is the model free-running amplitude. No stochastic noise is included in these maps.}

\subsection{Fourier-Domain Extraction of Modulation Response}

\textcolor{red}{Throughout this Supplementary Material, $\Delta\omega=2\pi(\nu_{\mathrm{remote}}-\nu_0)$ and $\Delta\nu=\nu_{\mathrm{remote}}-\nu_0=\Delta\omega/(2\pi)$, where $\nu_0$ is the free-running optical frequency of the injected laser in the absence of external optical injection. The remote-carrier frequency is $\nu_{\mathrm{remote}}$; $\Delta\omega$ is in $\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$ and $\Delta\nu$ is in Hz.}

For each operating point $(\Delta\nu, R_{\mathrm{inj}}, f_{\mathrm{mod}})$, the rate equations are first integrated through a transient interval, and the modulation response is extracted from the steady-state measurement window. The simulated injected-laser field is written as
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{red}{E_{\mathrm{injected}}}(t)=\mathrm{Re}\{\textcolor{red}{E_{\mathrm{injected}}}(t)\}+i\mathrm{Im}\{\textcolor{red}{E_{\mathrm{injected}}}(t)\}.
\end{equation}

The amplitude response channel is defined as the normalized field-amplitude fluctuation,
\begin{equation}
a_s(t)=\frac{|\textcolor{red}{E_{\mathrm{injected}}}(t)|}{\langle |\textcolor{red}{E_{\mathrm{injected}}}(t)| \rangle}-1,
\end{equation}
where $\langle \cdot \rangle$ denotes the time average over the measurement window. The phase response channel is obtained from the optical phase,
\begin{equation}
\phi_s(t)=\mathrm{unwrap}\left[\arg(\textcolor{red}{E_{\mathrm{injected}}}(t))\right].
\end{equation}
For the modulation-response extraction, the unwrapped phase traces are linearly detrended over the measurement window to remove any residual average phase drift before extracting the modulation component.

The fundamental response amplitude at the imposed modulation frequency is extracted by projecting each input and output waveform onto the sine and cosine components at $f_{\mathrm{mod}}$. Specifically, for a waveform $y(t)$ sampled over the measurement window, the amplitude at $f_{\mathrm{mod}}$ is obtained from the quadrature components at angular frequency $2\pi f_{\mathrm{mod}}$. This procedure is equivalent to extracting the Fourier component at the imposed modulation frequency and avoids using broadband spectral features unrelated to the driven response. The resulting input and output amplitudes are denoted as $A_{\mathrm{in}}(f_{\mathrm{mod}})$ and $A_{\mathrm{out}}(f_{\mathrm{mod}})$, respectively.

The response is reported in decibels as
\begin{equation}
H_{\mathrm{dB}}(f_{\mathrm{mod}})
=
20\log_{10}
\left[
\frac{
A_{\mathrm{out}}(f_{\mathrm{mod}})
}{
A_{\mathrm{in}}(f_{\mathrm{mod}})
}
\right].
\end{equation}

For AM-driven injection, $A_{\mathrm{in}}$ is extracted from the normalized remote amplitude modulation. For PM-driven injection, $A_{\mathrm{in}}$ is extracted from the unwrapped and linearly detrended remote phase modulation. The same procedure is applied to the corresponding AM and PM output channels to obtain the AM$\rightarrow$AM, AM$\rightarrow$PM, PM$\rightarrow$AM, and PM$\rightarrow$PM responses.

\section{Time-Domain Illustration of Modulation Channels}

Figure~\ref{fig:supp-am-pm-time} illustrates the time-domain input and output channels used for extracting the modulation response. The traces are calculated at a representative operating point with $\alpha=5$, $R_{\mathrm{inj}}=-50~\mathrm{dB}$, zero detuning, and $f_{\mathrm{mod}}=2~\mathrm{GHz}$. After a transient interval, the last eight modulation cycles are plotted for modulation depths from 0.60 to 1.00. For AM-driven injection, the input channel is the normalized source-field amplitude modulation, and the output channels are the normalized injected-laser field amplitude and the injected-laser field phase. These channels correspond to the AM$\rightarrow$AM and AM$\rightarrow$PM responses. For PM-driven injection, the input channel is the source-field phase modulation with depth $m_\phi=0.6\pi$ to $1.0\pi$, and the output channels are again the injected-laser field amplitude and phase, corresponding to the PM$\rightarrow$AM and PM$\rightarrow$PM responses. Thus, the figure provides a direct time-domain visualization of the channels used in the Fourier-domain extraction procedure described above.

This figure also provides additional detail for the nonlinear phase-modulation behavior discussed in the main text. In the small-signal regime, increasing modulation depth would primarily scale the response amplitude. However, for PM modulation depths of approximately $0.6\pi$ and above, the PM$\rightarrow$AM and PM$\rightarrow$PM traces show changes in waveform shape and relative amplitude that are not simple proportional rescalings. These time-domain traces therefore help visualize the onset of nonlinear response at large PM depth.
\textcolor{red}{The dimensionless PM depth is $m_\phi=\Delta\phi/\pi$; thus $m_\phi=0.6$--1.0 corresponds to a phase excursion amplitude $\Delta\phi=0.6\pi$--$1.0\pi$. Waveform distortion alone does not identify a bifurcation.}

\subsection{\textcolor{red}{Complex-Field Readout and Facet Reflection}}
\textcolor{red}{Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:review-reflection} uses the same inputs as main-text Fig.~6: independent generators with seeds 4 and 8 for the remote and local paths, a 20~ns CW settling interval, and 200 active encoded symbols at 10~Gbaud. Each antipodal pair encodes one independent data magnitude, corresponding to a 5~Gbaud data-symbol rate. The zero-order-held drives are filtered by the 20~GHz Gaussian FIR filter and sampled at 64 points per encoded symbol. Both figures display the first 100 active symbols (20--30~ns).}

\textcolor{red}{The local remodulator acts on the complete complex rate-equation output, including residual modulation. With an additive coherent reflected field, the model is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
E_{\mathrm{out}}(t)&=E_{\mathrm{injected}}(t)+\sqrt{R_f}E_{\mathrm{inj}}(t),\\
E_{\mathrm{injected}}^{(\mathrm{modulated})}(t)
&=\frac{E_{\mathrm{out}}(t)}{\langle E_{\mathrm{injected}}\rangle_{\mathrm{CW}}}\,y(t).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $R_f=0$ or 0.30 is a power reflectivity; the reflected field coefficient is therefore $\sqrt{R_f}$. The injection is $E_{\mathrm{inj}}=A_{\mathrm{fr}}10^{R_{\mathrm{inj,dB}}/20}[1+x(t)]$, while the computational data arm is the signed field $E_{\mathrm{remote}}^{(\mathrm{modulated})}=x(t)$. The normalization is the complex mean of the no-reflection field over 10--19~ns, before modulation, and is shared by both reflection cases without fitting. Fields use the same effective output-plane units with unit intracavity-to-output conversion. This additive comparison keeps the laser parameters fixed; device-specific facet coupling and output conversion would require calibration.}

\textcolor{red}{Balanced detection and sample-resolved accumulation are
\begin{equation}
D_j=2\operatorname{Re}\!\left\{x_j\left[E_{\mathrm{injected},j}^{(\mathrm{modulated})}\right]^*\right\},
\qquad A_n=\frac{1}{64}\sum_{j=1}^{n}D_j,
\end{equation}
starting at 20~ns. At each symbol end, this is the cumulative sum of symbol-averaged products. The ideal coherent reference uses the same filtered inputs and $D_j^{\mathrm{ideal}}=2x_jy_j$. No samples are omitted in the field, differential-signal, or accumulation traces. The mapping $(1+\operatorname{Re}E)/2$ in main-text Fig.~6b is for display only and is not used for multiplication. Reflection may increase an error or partly cancel it in an individual trajectory; such cancellation does not establish improved performance.}

\subsection{\textcolor{red}{Accumulated-Error Statistics over 1000 Inputs}}
\textcolor{red}{We extend the calculation to $Q=1000$ independent remote/local input pairs, each containing 1024 active encoded symbols. All conditions reuse the same pairs, with distinct remote and local vectors: $\alpha=0,2,5$, injection ratios $-50$, $-40$, and $-30$~dB, and $R_f=0$ or 0.30, at zero detuning. The 100\% field-modulation depth, eight-level magnitudes with antipodal pairing, filtering, sampling, and 20~ns CW preamble are unchanged. Each length $L$ is a prefix of the same trajectory, without restarting the laser or drawing new inputs.}

\textcolor{red}{For input pair $q$, the error and ensemble RMSE after $L$ encoded symbols are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\epsilon_q(L)&=A_{\mathrm{OIL},q}(L)-A_{\mathrm{ideal},q}(L),\\
A_{\mathrm{OIL},q}(L)&=\frac{1}{64}\sum_{j=1}^{64L}D_{q,j},\qquad
\mathrm{RMSE}(L)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{Q}\sum_q\epsilon_q(L)^2}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The ideal reference uses identical filtered waveforms and perfectly coherent clean carriers. These metrics quantify additional OIL/reflection error, excluding filtering error relative to the unfiltered digital dot product.}

\textcolor{red}{Both panels of Fig.~\ref{fig:review-statistics} use the same final-error percentage:
\begin{equation}
S=\sqrt{\frac{1}{Q}\sum_q A_{\mathrm{ideal},q}(1024)^2}=27.7159,
\qquad p_q=100\frac{\epsilon_q(1024)}{S}.
\end{equation}
Thus $S$ is the RMS scale of all 1000 ideal endpoints, not an error. Each signed error is divided by this common scale and multiplied by 100. The same denominator is used for all parameter/reflection conditions, preserving distribution shape and avoiding division by individual near-zero ideal results. A value of $p_q=1\%$ means an error of $0.01S$, not 1\% of that trial's ideal result. Gaussian fits describe the histograms but do not establish Gaussianity.}

\textcolor{red}{Figure~\ref{fig:review-statistics-metrics} also reports
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{NRMSE}(L)&=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_q\epsilon_q(L)^2}{\sum_q A_{\mathrm{ideal},q}(L)^2}},\\
r_{\mathrm P}(L)&=\operatorname{corr}_q[A_{\mathrm{OIL},q}(L),A_{\mathrm{ideal},q}(L)].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
NRMSE is displayed as a percentage. Pearson correlation measures covariation across input pairs and must be interpreted together with NRMSE: high correlation alone does not imply small numerical error. These are deterministic calculations without detector or laser noise.}

\textcolor{red}{The dependence on $\alpha$ reflects coupled amplitude/phase dynamics and coherent readout. Writing the calibrated no-reflection field as $E_{\mathrm{injected}}/\langle E_{\mathrm{injected}}\rangle_{\mathrm{CW}}=(1+a_\alpha)e^{i\phi_\alpha}$ gives
\begin{equation}
\delta D_\alpha=2xy[(1+a_\alpha)\cos\phi_\alpha-1]
\simeq 2xy\left(a_\alpha-\frac{\phi_\alpha^2}{2}\right).
\end{equation}
Here $a_\alpha$ and $\phi_\alpha$ are residual field-amplitude and phase fluctuations after CW calibration. Small AM affects the aligned readout to first order, whereas small PM enters to second order. Nonzero $\alpha$ couples amplitude and phase,\cite{Lau2009InjectionTutorial} but does not guarantee lower error. In the sampled conditions, RMSE decreases for nonzero $\alpha$ at $-50$ and $-40$~dB, while this trend reverses at $-30$~dB, especially for $\alpha=5$. The simulations retain the full nonlinear rate equations and exact readout; the small-residual expansion only explains sensitivity.}

\subsection{\textcolor{red}{Output Phase Control and Frequency-Drift Sensitivity}}
\textcolor{red}{Figure~\ref{fig:review-phase} evaluates a proportional--integral--derivative (PID) phase controller placed after local remodulation and before interference. The rate equations retain the time-dependent detuning term $-i2\pi\Delta\nu(t)E_{\mathrm{injected}}$. For a common received phase $\phi_c$ and a differential-path perturbation $\phi_d$, the fields are
\[
\begin{aligned}
E_{\mathrm{remote}}^{(\mathrm{modulated})}&=x e^{i\phi_c},\\
E_{\mathrm{injected}}^{(\mathrm{modulated})}&=\frac{E_{\mathrm{injected}}}{\langle E_{\mathrm{injected}}\rangle_{\mathrm{CW}}}\,y e^{i(\phi_d+u_{\mathrm{PID}})}.
\end{aligned}
\]
with the same fixed CW-field normalization $\langle E_{\mathrm{injected}}\rangle_{\mathrm{CW}}$ and differential readout defined above. The controller changes only the output phase; it does not actuate the laser frequency. These calculations use $\alpha=0$, $R_f=0$, and injection ratios of $-50$, $-40$, and $-30$~dB.}

\textcolor{red}{The auxiliary phase discriminator is idealized: it removes the data modulation and averages the carrier coherence over 5~ns, followed by a 10~MHz low-pass response; independent 3~mrad RMS readout noise is added before this filter. The PID updates every 5~ns and drives a first-order 20~MHz phase actuator with an additional 10~ns command delay and a $\pm4\pi$ range. For design crossover parameters $f_c=0.2,1,2$~MHz, the gains are $K_p=0.15$, $K_i=2\pi f_c\sqrt{1-K_p^2}$, and $K_d=0.015/(2\pi f_c)$, with 10~MHz derivative filtering and anti-windup. These are illustrative controller parameters, not measured hardware bandwidths. An experimental implementation requires a suitable reference or pilot discriminator; low-pass filtering the computational BPD signal alone does not necessarily separate phase drift from data.}

\textcolor{red}{The original 10~Gbaud, 100\%-depth, Gaussian-filtered encoding is retained at 64 samples per symbol. Following the 20~ns CW preamble, the laser and controller evolve continuously over 256 consecutive 1024-symbol windows. The final-result NRMSE uses the 207 windows centered after 5~$\mu$s, with identical inputs for every comparison; these are consecutive windows of one input pair, not the 1000 independent pairs of Figs.~\ref{fig:review-statistics} and \ref{fig:review-statistics-metrics}. The ideal reference remains $D_{\mathrm{ideal}}=2xy$ for the same filtered waveforms.}

\textcolor{red}{At $-50$~dB, the $f_c=1$~MHz controller reduces final-result NRMSE from 22.26\% to 1.96\% for the slower phase perturbations, but a further 20~MHz disturbance leaves 8.53\% error. For the 30~MHz-amplitude detuning variation, NRMSE decreases from 5.97\% to 0.53\%. These synthetic perturbations do not represent measured fiber noise. Output PID cannot restore cavity locking outside the locking interval: current/temperature feedback is separately required,\cite{Liu2015HomodyneOIL} along with controlled polarization and path matching for output phase stabilization.\cite{Liu2014ModulatorFreeQAM}}
